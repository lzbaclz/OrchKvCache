\begin{abstract}
Long-context large language models (LLMs) are bottlenecked by KV-cache memory
that grows linearly with sequence length, forcing operators to either
over-provision expensive GPU memory or sacrifice output quality through lossy
eviction.  Through a measurement study of three model families---Qwen2.5-7B,
Llama-3.1-8B, and Mistral-7B---we discover that attention-weight distributions
exhibit \emph{stable hot-set concentrations} (Gini coefficient ${>}\,0.91$
across all models) and \emph{predictable reuse distances} (mean 1.17 steps for
hot blocks), creating an opportunity for lossless offloading that prior
heuristic policies fail to exploit.  We present \sys{}, a tiered KV-cache
manager that combines an exponential-moving-average reuse-distance predictor
(Jaccard\,$=$\,0.66 vs.\ InfiniGen cross-layer 0.46 at 2K tokens) with a
critical-path-aware scheduler to place each cache block on the right tier
(GPU, DRAM, or SSD) at the right time.  The scheduler guarantees that
promotion latency (P50\,$=$\,46\,$\mu$s, P99\,$=$\,250\,$\mu$s) is hidden
behind decode-phase compute.  In our evaluation across 1{,}350 prompts from
LongBench, RULER, and ShareGPT, \sys{} achieves 100\% bit-exact output
match with full-GPU serving, maintains service at 10\% GPU KV budget with
only 7\% throughput loss, adds only 1.1\% overhead when adding the SSD tier,
and enables a theoretical 5.26$\times$ concurrency improvement by offloading
cold blocks.  The $N{=}10$ periodic attention sampling mode achieves 89\%
classification accuracy with approximately zero overhead compared to
attention-free decoding.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The memory wall is the binding constraint on long-context LLM serving.
During auto-regressive inference, every generated token requires the full
key--value (KV) cache of all previously generated tokens across all
transformer layers.  For a model such as Llama-3.1-8B with 32 layers and
grouped-query attention (GQA, 8 KV heads, head dimension 128), each token
adds 128\,KB of KV state; a single 128K-context request thus requires
${\sim}$16\,GB of KV cache alone, consuming a substantial fraction of an
A100-80GB GPU's VRAM.  Operators face a stark trade-off: either provision
enough GPU memory for worst-case context lengths---incurring prohibitive
cost---or limit batch sizes, throttling throughput and underutilising
expensive compute.

The natural remedy is to offload cold KV blocks to cheaper memory tiers
(host DRAM and NVMe SSD), keeping only the hot working set on GPU.  Yet
prior offloading systems face a \emph{lossless paradox}: if a block is
needed for the next decode step but resides on a remote tier, the decode
step stalls until promotion completes; alternatively, some systems resort to
lossy eviction (dropping tokens permanently), which degrades quality on
recall-intensive tasks.  Neither outcome is acceptable for production serving.

Our work begins with a measurement study (\S\ref{sec:measurement}) of three
representative model families---Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, and Mistral-7B---across
diverse workloads.  We observe three findings that motivate a new design:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Extreme attention concentration.}
    Over 80--92\% of total attention mass concentrates on the top 10\% of
    KV blocks.  Gini coefficients exceed 0.91 for all tested models,
    indicating that most blocks are rarely accessed during any given decode
    step.

  \item \textbf{Predictable hot-set membership.}
    The set of hot blocks exhibits high temporal stability: inter-step
    Jaccard similarity ranges from 0.77 (Llama-3.1-8B) to 0.90 (Qwen2.5-7B),
    making reuse-distance prediction feasible with lightweight signals.

  \item \textbf{Promotion latency fits within decode slack.}
    A typical decode step on Qwen2.5-7B takes 25.4\,ms under eager attention,
    while DRAM$\to$GPU promotion completes in 46\,$\mu$s (P50) and
    SSD$\to$DRAM$\to$GPU promotion completes in under 500\,$\mu$s---orders
    of magnitude shorter than the decode step, providing ample slack for
    overlapping data movement with compute.
\end{enumerate}

No prior system combines reuse-distance prediction, critical-path awareness,
and three-tier storage in a single framework.  LMCache~\cite{yao2024lmcache}
focuses on \emph{cross-query} prefix reuse (a complementary problem to
\sys{}'s \emph{intra-request} residency scheduling).
InfiniGen~\cite{lee2024infinigen} predicts hot blocks via cross-layer
attention correlation, but operates only on GPU+DRAM and lacks
critical-path guarantees.  FlexGen~\cite{sheng2023flexgen} provides
three-tier offloading but uses static, offline scheduling.

We make the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Workload characterisation} (\S\ref{sec:measurement}):
    a measurement study of attention concentration, hot-set stability,
    reuse-distance distributions, and promotion slack across three model
    families and multiple workloads, establishing the empirical basis for
    lossless offloading.

  \item \textbf{Critical-path residency model} (\S\ref{sec:model}):
    a formal condition under which a KV block can be safely offloaded to
    DRAM or SSD without risking a critical-path stall, grounded in the
    measured promotion latencies and decode-step compute times.

  \item \textbf{System design and implementation} (\S\ref{sec:design}--\S\ref{sec:impl}):
    \sys{}, comprising a low-overhead signal acquisition layer, an
    EMA-based reuse-distance predictor, a critical-path-aware scheduler,
    and a three-tier storage backend, implemented in ${\sim}$4{,}500 lines
    of C/CUDA and ${\sim}$1{,}200 lines of Python.

  \item \textbf{Evaluation} (\S\ref{sec:eval}):
    across 1{,}350 prompts from LongBench, RULER, and ShareGPT, \sys{}
    achieves 100\% bit-exact output match, maintains service at 10\% GPU
    KV budget with 7\% throughput loss, and keeps promotion P99 below
    250\,$\mu$s across all memory budgets.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Motivation}\label{sec:background}

% --- 2.1 ---
\subsection{The KV Cache Memory Bottleneck}\label{sec:bg:bottleneck}

Auto-regressive LLM inference proceeds in two phases.  During
\emph{prefill}, all prompt tokens are processed in parallel, producing a KV
cache entry for each token at each transformer layer.  During \emph{decode},
one token is generated per step; each step appends one new KV entry and
attends to the entire accumulated KV cache.

The KV cache size scales as $O(n \cdot d_\text{kv} \cdot L)$, where $n$ is
the sequence length, $d_\text{kv} = 2 \cdot n_\text{kv\_heads} \cdot
d_\text{head}$ is the per-layer KV dimension, and $L$ is the number of
layers.  Table~\ref{tab:kv_sizing} shows the per-token KV cost for the three
models used in this study.  Even with GQA (Qwen2.5-7B: 4 KV heads;
Llama-3.1-8B and Mistral-7B: 8 KV heads), the cumulative KV footprint
becomes substantial at long context lengths.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Per-token KV cache cost for the three evaluated models (FP16).}
\label{tab:kv_sizing}
\begin{tabular}{lcccr}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{Layers} & \textbf{KV Heads} & $\mathbf{d_\text{head}}$ &
  \textbf{KV/token} \\
\midrule
Qwen2.5-7B   & 28 & 4  & 128 & 56\,KB \\
Llama-3.1-8B & 32 & 8  & 128 & 128\,KB \\
Mistral-7B   & 32 & 8  & 128 & 128\,KB \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The operational consequence is that batch size is \emph{memory-capped}:
the GPU can only serve as many concurrent requests as fit in VRAM after
model weights and activations.  This under-utilises compute, leading to
low goodput.  Under memory pressure, vLLM~\cite{kwon2023vllm} preempts
entire requests via sequence-level swapping, a coarse mechanism that
hurts tail latency.

% --- 2.2 ---
\subsection{The Lossless Offloading Paradox}\label{sec:bg:paradox}

We define \emph{lossless} serving as producing bit-exact output tokens
identical to those produced by full-GPU serving with no memory pressure.
Any token eviction, quantisation, or attention approximation is \emph{lossy}.

Offloading---moving KV blocks to DRAM or SSD---preserves losslessness only
if every block is available on GPU when the attention kernel needs it.  If
a block resides on a remote tier at the moment of access, the decode step
\emph{stalls} until the promotion transfer completes, violating time-per-output-token (TPOT) SLO targets.  Prior systems handle this dilemma in
different ways:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Lossy eviction} (H2O~\cite{zhang2024h2o},
    StreamingLLM~\cite{xiao2024streamingllm},
    ScissorHands~\cite{liu2024scissorhands}, SnapKV~\cite{li2024snapkv}):
    permanently drop low-attention tokens.  Quality degrades on
    recall-heavy tasks such as needle-in-a-haystack retrieval.

  \item \textbf{Offload with heuristic promotion}
    (InfiniGen~\cite{lee2024infinigen}, FlexGen~\cite{sheng2023flexgen}):
    move cold blocks to DRAM or SSD but rely on coarse heuristics (LRU,
    cross-layer speculation) for deciding \emph{when} to promote, leading
    to unpredictable stalls.

  \item \textbf{Cross-query caching} (LMCache~\cite{yao2024lmcache},
    CacheBlend~\cite{yao2024cacheblend}): cache KV across queries sharing
    common prefixes, reducing TTFT.  This is complementary to intra-request
    residency scheduling.

  \item \textbf{Quantised caching} (KIVI~\cite{liu2024kivi}): compress KV to
    fewer bits; still lossy and accumulates error over long contexts.
\end{itemize}

The gap is clear: no system provides a principled, measurement-grounded
policy that achieves lossless offloading while guaranteeing that promotion
latency does not land on the critical path.

\section{Measurement Study}\label{sec:measurement}

We profile three model families---Qwen2.5-7B (28 layers, 4 KV heads),
Llama-3.1-8B (32 layers, 8 KV heads), and Mistral-7B (32 layers, 8 KV
heads)---under diverse workloads using an instrumented HuggingFace
Transformers inference loop with eager attention.  We collect per-layer,
per-head attention weights at each decode step and compute block-level
statistics with a block size of 16 tokens.

\paragraph{Workloads.}
We use ShareGPT (multi-turn conversation traces), LongBench (multi-document
QA and summarisation), and RULER (synthetic needle-in-a-haystack at
controlled lengths of 1K, 2K, and 4K tokens).  Each experiment profiles
8 prompts per model--workload combination with up to 64 decode steps.

% --- 3.1 ---
\subsection{Attention Concentration}\label{sec:meas:hotset}

\paragraph{Finding 1: Extreme skew.}
Across all three models and workloads, attention weights exhibit extreme
inequality.  Table~\ref{tab:gini} reports the Gini coefficient of the
per-block attention mass distribution, averaged across layers and prompts.
All models achieve Gini $> 0.91$, indicating that a small fraction of blocks
captures the vast majority of attention mass.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Attention concentration metrics across models
(mean $\pm$ std over 8 prompts, 1024-token context, 48 decode steps).}
\label{tab:gini}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{Gini} & \textbf{Top-10\% Conc.} & \textbf{Jaccard} \\
\midrule
Qwen2.5-7B    & 0.92 $\pm$ 0.02 & 0.92 & 0.90 \\
Llama-3.1-8B  & 0.91 $\pm$ 0.03 & 0.87 & 0.77 \\
Mistral-7B    & 0.91 $\pm$ 0.02 & 0.85 & 0.83 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The top-10\% concentration column reports the fraction of total attention
mass captured by the top 10\% of KV blocks.  The values range from 0.85
to 0.92, confirming that 80--92\% of attention mass is concentrated on
fewer than 10\% of blocks.

\paragraph{Layer heterogeneity.}
Concentration is not uniform across layers.  Shallow layers (layers 0--5)
tend to exhibit more diffuse attention patterns, while deeper layers
(layers 20+) show sharper concentration.  This motivates per-layer
budgeting in the scheduler.

% --- 3.2 ---
\subsection{Hot-Set Stability and Reuse Distance}\label{sec:meas:reuse}

\paragraph{Finding 2: Temporal predictability.}
We define the \emph{hot set} at decode step $t$ as the set of KV blocks
whose per-block attention mass exceeds the 90th percentile.  The Jaccard
similarity between consecutive hot sets (column ``Jaccard'' in
Table~\ref{tab:gini}) ranges from 0.77 (Llama-3.1-8B) to 0.90 (Qwen2.5-7B).
This high stability means that knowing which blocks were hot at step $t$
provides a strong prior for step $t{+}1$.

\paragraph{Reuse distance.}
We define \emph{block reuse distance} as the number of decode steps between
consecutive accesses to the same KV block (where ``access'' means the block
is in the hot set).  For hot blocks, the mean reuse distance is 1.17 steps,
indicating that hot blocks are accessed nearly every step.  Cold blocks
exhibit much longer reuse distances, with the cold tail (blocks never in the
hot set during the observation window) comprising 30--60\% of all blocks
depending on the workload.

This heavy-tailed reuse-distance distribution is the key enabler: a policy
that can distinguish hot blocks (reuse distance $\approx 1$) from cold
blocks (reuse distance $\gg 1$) can offload the cold tail with near-zero
risk of stall.

% --- 3.3 ---
\subsection{Promotion Latency and Overlap Slack}\label{sec:meas:slack}

\paragraph{Finding 3: Promotion fits in decode slack.}
We measure promotion latency via asynchronous CUDA DMA transfers
(\texttt{cudaMemcpyAsync} with pinned host memory) for DRAM$\to$GPU, and
two-hop SSD$\to$DRAM$\to$GPU via \texttt{O\_DIRECT} reads followed by DMA.
Table~\ref{tab:latency} summarises the measured latencies for 32\,KB blocks
(16 tokens $\times$ one KV-head group in Qwen2.5-7B).

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Promotion latency per 32\,KB KV block and decode-step compute time.}
\label{tab:latency}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Metric} & \textbf{P50} & \textbf{P99} \\
\midrule
DRAM$\to$GPU promotion    & 46\,$\mu$s   & 250\,$\mu$s  \\
SSD$\to$DRAM$\to$GPU (two-hop) & ${\sim}$500\,$\mu$s & ${\sim}$1\,ms \\
Decode step (Qwen2.5-7B, eager) & \multicolumn{2}{c}{25.4\,ms} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The decode-step compute time of 25.4\,ms exceeds the DRAM promotion P99
(250\,$\mu$s) by over 100$\times$ and the SSD two-hop promotion by over
25$\times$.  This implies that promotions can be fully hidden behind decode
compute via pipelining, as long as they are initiated sufficiently early.

% --- 3.4 ---
\subsection{Implications for System Design}\label{sec:meas:implications}

The three findings collectively define the design space:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Concentration $\to$ small hot set.} Only 10--20\% of blocks
    need to reside on GPU at any time; the rest can be offloaded.

  \item \textbf{Temporal stability $\to$ predictable membership.}  An
    exponential moving average (EMA) of past attention scores is
    sufficient to predict next-step hot-set membership.

  \item \textbf{Large overlap slack $\to$ safe offloading.}  Both DRAM and
    SSD offloading are safe if the block's predicted reuse distance exceeds
    2 steps ($2 \times 25.4\,\text{ms} \gg 500\,\mu$s).

  \item \textbf{Workload variation $\to$ adaptive policy.}  Retrieval-heavy
    tasks (RULER) exhibit sharper concentration; summarisation tasks
    (LongBench multi\_news) have broader attention.  A single static
    threshold cannot cover all workloads.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Critical-Path Residency Model}\label{sec:model}

The goal of the residency model is: given measured signals, derive a
per-block placement decision that guarantees (a)~lossless output and
(b)~zero critical-path stall, while minimising GPU memory usage.

% --- 4.1 ---
\subsection{Reuse-Distance Prediction}\label{sec:model:reuse}

We predict block importance using an exponential moving average (EMA) of
attention-derived scores.  Let $a_t(b)$ denote the attention mass of block
$b$ at decode step $t$, aggregated across heads.  The EMA score is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ema}
  \text{ema}_t(b) = \lambda \cdot \text{ema}_{t-1}(b) + (1-\lambda) \cdot a_t(b),
\end{equation}
where $\lambda = 0.9$ is the retention factor (90\% old, 10\% new).  Blocks
with high EMA scores are classified as \emph{hot}; blocks with low scores
as \emph{cold}.

The composite hotness score fuses three signals:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hotness}
  S(b) = \alpha \cdot \hat{a}(b) + \beta \cdot R(b) + \gamma \cdot F(b),
\end{equation}
where $\hat{a}(b)$ is the min-max normalised EMA score, $R(b) =
\exp(-\tau \cdot (t - t_{\text{last}}(b)))$ captures recency via a
pre-computed 256-entry lookup table, and $F(b)$ is the normalised access
frequency.  Default weights are $\alpha=0.7$, $\beta=0.2$, $\gamma=0.1$.

The first four tokens (attention sinks~\cite{xiao2024streamingllm}) are
unconditionally classified as hot.  The remaining blocks are partitioned
into hot/warm/cold by thresholds $\theta_\text{hot}$ and
$\theta_\text{cold}$ that adapt to GPU utilisation
(\S\ref{sec:design:scheduler}).

\paragraph{Predictor quality.}
On Qwen2.5-7B with 2K-token prompts, the EMA predictor achieves a
Jaccard similarity of 0.66 between predicted and actual hot sets, compared
to 0.46 for the InfiniGen cross-layer predictor under the same conditions.
The EMA predictor benefits from temporal continuity (same layer, consecutive
steps) rather than spatial transfer (adjacent layers, same step).

\paragraph{Complexity.}
The EMA update is $O(B)$ per decode step, where $B$ is the number of active
blocks.  Per-block state is 4 bytes (16-bit EMA score + 16-bit last-access
timestamp), totalling 4\,MB for 1M blocks.

% --- 4.2 ---
\subsection{Promotion Latency Model}\label{sec:model:latency}

Let $\ell_T(b)$ denote the promotion latency for block $b$ from tier $T$.
Based on micro-benchmarks (\S\ref{sec:meas:slack}):
\begin{align}
  \ell_\text{DRAM}(b) &\approx 46\,\mu\text{s (P50)}, \quad 250\,\mu\text{s (P99)}, \\
  \ell_\text{SSD}(b)  &\approx 500\,\mu\text{s (P50)}, \quad 1\,\text{ms (P99)}.
\end{align}

The \emph{overlap slack} $\Delta^{(t)}$ is the decode-step compute time
available for hiding promotion.  For Qwen2.5-7B, $\Delta \approx 25.4$\,ms.
The slack depends on model size, batch size, and hardware; we measure it
once per configuration during a calibration phase.

% --- 4.3 ---
\subsection{Offload Safety Condition}\label{sec:model:offload}

Block $b$ can be safely offloaded to tier $T$ if and only if its predicted
reuse distance $\hat{r}_b$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:offload}
  \hat{r}_b > \left\lceil \frac{\ell_T(b)}{\Delta} \right\rceil + m,
\end{equation}
where $m \geq 1$ is a safety margin.  This condition guarantees that
promotion can be initiated at least $\lceil \ell_T / \Delta \rceil + m$
steps before the block is next accessed, and the transfer completes before
the decode step that needs the block.

For our measured values: DRAM offloading is safe when $\hat{r}_b > 2$ steps
(since $250\,\mu\text{s} \ll 25.4\,\text{ms}$, a single step provides
sufficient slack).  SSD offloading is also safe when $\hat{r}_b > 2$ steps
(since $500\,\mu\text{s} \ll 25.4\,\text{ms}$).  In practice, the safety
margin $m=1$ provides an additional buffer.

\paragraph{Tier selection.}
Warm blocks (moderate reuse distance) are offloaded to DRAM; cold blocks
(long reuse distance) are further demoted to SSD.  When a cold block's
predicted reuse distance shortens (indicating imminent access), it is
promoted back through the SSD$\to$DRAM$\to$GPU pipeline.

\paragraph{Rescue path.}
If a block is accessed earlier than predicted (prediction error), the
scheduler triggers an \emph{emergency synchronous promotion} that preempts
lower-priority transfers.  In our evaluation, rescue events are rare:
the EMA predictor's high Jaccard similarity (0.66--0.90) keeps the
misprediction rate low.

\section{System Design}\label{sec:design}

% --- 5.1 ---
\subsection{Architecture Overview}\label{sec:design:arch}

\sys{} comprises four components arranged in a per-decode-step control loop:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Signal Acquisition Layer:}
    extracts per-block importance signals from the attention computation,
    supporting three modes: full attention extraction (oracle), $N$-step
    periodic sampling, and QK inner-product proxy.

  \item \textbf{Reuse-Distance Predictor:}
    maintains per-block EMA scores (Eq.~\ref{eq:ema}) and classifies blocks
    into hot/warm/cold tiers using the composite score (Eq.~\ref{eq:hotness}).

  \item \textbf{Critical-Path Scheduler:}
    applies the offload safety condition (Eq.~\ref{eq:offload}) to decide
    which blocks to offload and when to initiate promotions, ensuring that
    no promotion lands on the critical path.

  \item \textbf{Tiered Storage Backend:}
    manages GPU HBM, host DRAM (pinned), and NVMe SSD pools, executing
    asynchronous DMA transfers via CUDA streams and \texttt{O\_DIRECT} I/O.
\end{enumerate}

After each decode step, the signal layer emits importance scores; the
predictor updates reuse estimates; the scheduler issues offload, promote,
or pin commands; and the backend executes asynchronous transfers, all
within the overlap slack of the subsequent decode step.

% --- 5.2 ---
\subsection{Low-Overhead Signal Acquisition}\label{sec:design:signal}

The cost of signal acquisition determines the practical overhead of the
entire system.  We support three modes with decreasing cost:

\paragraph{Full attention (oracle).}
Setting \texttt{output\_attentions=True} in the transformer forward pass
captures the complete softmax attention distribution at each layer.  This
provides the highest-quality signal but incurs overhead: on Qwen2.5-7B with
512-token prompts, full attention extraction adds overhead to each decode
step.

\paragraph{$N$-step periodic sampling.}
Attention weights are collected every $N$ decode steps; between samples,
the predictor reuses the most recent observation.  With $N{=}10$, the
sampling mode achieves 89\% classification accuracy (relative to the
full-attention oracle) with approximately zero amortised overhead, because
only 1 in 10 steps pays the extraction cost.

\paragraph{QK inner-product proxy.}
A lightweight proxy computes $\max_{j \in \text{block}} q_t^T k_j$ for each
block using only the current query vector and the top-$k$ keys per block.
This avoids the full softmax computation entirely.

% --- 5.3 ---
\subsection{Reuse-Distance-Aware Predictor}\label{sec:design:predictor}

The predictor maintains per-block state: a 16-bit EMA score and a 16-bit
last-access timestamp, for a total of 4 bytes per block.  At 16 tokens
per block for a 32K-token context, this amounts to 2{,}048 blocks $\times$
4 bytes = 8\,KB of predictor state---negligible overhead.

The hot/warm/cold classification thresholds adapt to workload-specific
reuse-distance distributions using online percentile tracking.  When GPU
utilisation exceeds a high-water mark, $\theta_\text{hot}$ is lowered to
classify more blocks as warm/cold and trigger offloading.  When utilisation
drops below a low-water mark, $\theta_\text{hot}$ is raised to retain more
blocks on GPU.  A 0.5-second cooldown timer prevents oscillation.

% --- 5.4 ---
\subsection{Critical-Path-Aware Scheduler}\label{sec:design:scheduler}

The scheduling algorithm proceeds in four phases per decode step:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Classify:} partition all active blocks into hot (keep on GPU),
    warm (candidate for DRAM offload), and cold (candidate for SSD offload)
    based on the composite hotness score.

  \item \textbf{Compute safe offload time:} for each warm/cold block, verify
    the offload safety condition (Eq.~\ref{eq:offload}).  Only offload if
    the predicted reuse distance provides sufficient margin.

  \item \textbf{Schedule promotions:} for blocks currently on DRAM or SSD
    whose predicted reuse distance is shortening (indicating imminent access),
    initiate asynchronous promotion transfers.  Promotions are scheduled
    $\hat{r}_b - \lceil \ell_T / \Delta \rceil$ steps before predicted access.

  \item \textbf{Priority arbitration:} when multiple promotions compete for
    DMA bandwidth, prioritise by soonest predicted access time.  An in-flight
    cap prevents bus saturation; a back-pressure mechanism pauses offloads
    when the promotion queue is full.
\end{enumerate}

The scheduling loop runs entirely on the CPU and completes in under
60\,$\mu$s for up to 4{,}096 active blocks, as measured by the C/CUDA
microbenchmark.

% --- 5.5 ---
\subsection{Tiered Storage Backend}\label{sec:design:backend}

The backend manages three I/O paths:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{DRAM tier:} pinned host memory allocated via
    \texttt{cudaMallocHost}.  Transfers use \texttt{cudaMemcpyAsync} on
    dedicated CUDA streams (4 streams, round-robin).  Non-blocking transfers
    with explicit \texttt{cudaStreamSynchronize} at the consumer.

  \item \textbf{SSD tier:} \texttt{O\_DIRECT} reads into pinned buffers,
    followed by DMA to GPU (two-hop).  Blocks are laid out contiguously
    on SSD in 2\,MB-aligned regions to match filesystem block size,
    using a pre-allocated file pool to avoid allocation jitter.

  \item \textbf{GDS path (optional):} on systems with NVIDIA GPUDirect
    Storage~\cite{nvidia2023gds} support, SSD$\to$GPU transfers bypass
    the CPU entirely.  The backend falls back to POSIX two-hop on systems
    without GDS.
\end{itemize}

Block writes to SSD align to 4\,KB page boundaries.  Batching 8+ blocks
per write achieves ${\sim}$40\% peak bandwidth utilisation, a 10$\times$
improvement over single-block writes.

% --- 5.6 ---
\subsection{Integration with vLLM}\label{sec:design:production}

\sys{} integrates with vLLM~\cite{kwon2023vllm}'s PagedAttention as a
drop-in replacement for the block allocator.  The tiered allocator intercepts
block allocation and deallocation calls, routing them to the appropriate
tier.  The scheduler hooks into vLLM's decode loop to receive importance
signals and issue offload/promote commands.  The only kernel modification is
the optional signal acquisition GEMV for the QK proxy mode.

We note that \sys{} and LMCache address different levels of the caching
hierarchy: LMCache handles \emph{cross-query} prefix reuse (reducing TTFT
by sharing KV across queries with common prefixes), while \sys{} handles
\emph{intra-request} residency scheduling (deciding which blocks of a single
request's KV cache reside on which tier during decode).  The two systems
are complementary and can be composed.

\section{Implementation}\label{sec:impl}

\sys{} is implemented in ${\sim}$4{,}500 lines of C/CUDA and
${\sim}$1{,}200 lines of Python.  The C/CUDA core
(\texttt{src/core/}, \texttt{src/tiered\_store/}, \texttt{src/scheduler/})
implements the KV block abstraction, the three-tier storage manager
(GPU slab pool, pinned DRAM pool, SSD-backed OrchFS), the eight scheduling
stages (attention tracker, hot-cold classifier, adaptive threshold, eviction
policy, prefetch scheduler, migration pipeline, and tiered manager), and the
asynchronous DMA engine.

The Python layer (\texttt{python/orchkv/}) provides the
\texttt{KVCacheManager} and \texttt{FastKVCacheManager} classes that
interface with HuggingFace Transformers' \texttt{DynamicCache}, translating
between the C API and PyTorch tensors.  Pybind11 bindings expose the C core
to Python.

We provide three evaluation paths, following the three-path claim separation
principle:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{C/CUDA microbenchmark:} validates scheduling overhead
    ($<$60\,$\mu$s at 4{,}096 blocks), async DMA correctness, and
    block-level locking.

  \item \textbf{Python prototype:} validates policy quality (Gini, Jaccard,
    reuse distance), lossless integrity (bit-exact match), and predictor
    comparison (EMA vs.\ InfiniGen).

  \item \textbf{vLLM integration:} validates production compatibility,
    reporting throughput, TTFT, TPOT, and preemption statistics under
    realistic serving load.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{vLLM Serving under Memory Pressure}\label{sec:eval:vllm}

To evaluate production-level impact, we run a ShareGPT-style serving
simulation with vLLM on Qwen2.5-7B.  We generate 50 requests with
Poisson-distributed arrivals and prompt lengths ranging from 200 to 2{,}000
tokens, generating 128 tokens per request.

\paragraph{Baseline throughput.}
Under generous memory allocation (85\% GPU memory utilisation), vLLM
achieves 340--2{,}315 tok/s depending on the batch composition.  This
represents the production throughput ceiling.

\paragraph{Memory pressure (batch=128, gmu=0.3).}
Under memory pressure with low GPU memory utilisation (gmu=0.3) and
large batch sizes, vLLM triggers preemption events---swapping entire
requests to host memory---causing TPOT spikes and throughput degradation.
\sys{}'s block-level offloading avoids this by proactively moving cold
blocks to DRAM/SSD, keeping the hot working set on GPU and eliminating
the need for sequence-level preemption.

\paragraph{Concurrency improvement.}
By offloading 80--90\% of KV blocks to DRAM/SSD (the cold tail identified
in \S\ref{sec:meas:reuse}), \sys{} theoretically enables a 5.26$\times$
concurrency improvement: the GPU memory freed by offloading cold blocks
can accommodate 5.26$\times$ more concurrent requests' hot sets.  This
projection assumes Qwen2.5-7B's measured attention concentration (92\% of
mass in 10\% of blocks) and is validated by the memory sweep results
in \S\ref{sec:eval:memory}.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{KV-Cache Eviction and Compression.}
H2O~\cite{zhang2024h2o} evicts tokens with the lowest accumulated attention
scores; StreamingLLM~\cite{xiao2024streamingllm} retains only attention sinks
and a sliding window; ScissorHands~\cite{liu2024scissorhands} and
SnapKV~\cite{li2024snapkv} exploit persistence of importance for
compression.  PyramidKV~\cite{cai2024pyramidkv} applies layer-wise
budgeting based on attention funnelling.  Ada-KV~\cite{feng2024adakv}
adaptively allocates eviction budgets.
All these methods are \emph{lossy}---they permanently discard KV entries,
degrading quality on recall-intensive tasks.  \sys{} is lossless: every
KV block is preserved and promoted back to GPU before access.
KIVI~\cite{liu2024kivi} compresses KV to 2 bits; this is complementary
and can be composed with \sys{} as an additional tier.

\paragraph{KV-Cache Offloading.}
FlexGen~\cite{sheng2023flexgen} enables three-tier offloading (GPU+DRAM+SSD)
but uses a static, offline schedule optimised for throughput-oriented batch
inference.  InfiniGen~\cite{lee2024infinigen} predicts important KV pages
using cross-layer attention correlation and speculatively prefetches them
from DRAM; it does not support SSD and lacks critical-path guarantees.
Our measurement shows that InfiniGen's cross-layer predictor achieves
Jaccard 0.46 vs.\ \sys{}'s EMA predictor at 0.66 on Qwen2.5-7B
(\S\ref{sec:eval:predictor}).
LMCache~\cite{yao2024lmcache} caches KV blocks across queries sharing
common prefixes, reducing TTFT; this addresses \emph{cross-query} reuse
and is complementary to \sys{}'s \emph{intra-request} residency scheduling.
FlexiCache~\cite{li2024flexicache} uses per-head temporal stability for
attention-aware offloading to DRAM.
CachedAttention~\cite{gao2024cachedattention} and
SqueezeAttention~\cite{yang2024squeezeattention} explore KV sharing and
layer-adaptive compression.
vTensor~\cite{xu2024vtensor} provides virtual tensor management for
flexible KV placement.

\paragraph{GPU--Storage Systems.}
BaM~\cite{qureshi2023bam} enables fine-grained GPU-orchestrated SSD access;
ALISA~\cite{zhao2024alisa} accelerates inference via sparsity-aware KV
caching; KVDrive~\cite{patel2024kvdrive} provides system-level SSD
offloading for KV cache.  These systems focus on hardware-level I/O
mechanisms without the KV-cache-specific reuse-distance modelling and
critical-path scheduling that \sys{} provides.
GPUDirect Storage~\cite{nvidia2023gds} enables direct SSD$\to$GPU
transfers; \sys{} supports GDS as an optional I/O backend
(\S\ref{sec:design:backend}).

\paragraph{Attention Kernels and Serving Systems.}
FlashAttention~\cite{dao2022flashattention} and its
successors~\cite{dao2023flashattention2, shah2024flashattention3} optimise
attention computation through IO-aware tiling.
FlashInfer~\cite{ye2024flashinfer} provides a customisable attention engine.
vLLM~\cite{kwon2023vllm} introduced PagedAttention for efficient GPU memory
management.
Quest~\cite{tang2024quest} uses QK page selection for sparse attention.
These are complementary: \sys{}'s residency scheduler can layer on top of
any attention kernel.

\paragraph{Multi-Tier and Multi-Tenant Systems.}
Mooncake~\cite{qiu2024mooncake} disaggregates KV cache across nodes;
Tutti~\cite{wu2024tutti} manages KV for multi-tenant serving.
\sys{} currently operates within a single node; cross-node extension
is future work.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Prediction accuracy under adversarial patterns.}
The EMA predictor assumes temporal continuity of hot-set membership.
For workloads with highly irregular attention patterns (e.g., adversarial
prompts designed to scatter attention), prediction accuracy may degrade.
The rescue path (\S\ref{sec:model:offload}) handles mispredictions, but
at the cost of synchronous promotion stalls.  In practice, such patterns
are rare in production workloads.

\paragraph{Long-context attention extraction.}
At sequence lengths $\geq$ 8K tokens, full attention extraction becomes
infeasible due to the quadratic memory requirement of the attention matrix.
The $N$-step sampling and QK proxy modes address this by avoiding full
attention materialisation, but the oracle (full attention) mode is limited
to shorter contexts.

\paragraph{Python prototype overhead.}
The current prototype's \texttt{build\_past\_kv()} call dominates per-step
overhead (${\sim}$48\,ms out of 81\,ms total), as it reconstructs the
KV cache tuple in Python on every step.  The C/CUDA scheduling core itself
runs in $<$60\,$\mu$s.  A production deployment would eliminate this
overhead by integrating the tiered allocator directly into the serving
engine's C++ block manager.

\paragraph{Single-GPU evaluation.}
All experiments use a single GPU.  Multi-GPU tensor-parallel and
pipeline-parallel deployments introduce cross-GPU KV cache synchronisation
challenges that we leave to future work.

\paragraph{Hardware requirements for SSD tier.}
The SSD tier requires NVMe SSDs with sufficient sequential read bandwidth
($\geq$3\,GB/s).  GDS support requires specific NVIDIA driver versions
and NVMe hardware.  The system falls back gracefully to POSIX
\texttt{O\_DIRECT} on systems without GDS.

\paragraph{Broader impact.}
By enabling longer-context serving on commodity hardware, \sys{} may
help democratise access to capable LLMs.  The system does not introduce
new capabilities to the underlying model; it only changes \emph{where}
KV data resides during inference.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented \sys{}, a tiered KV-cache residency scheduler that combines
workload characterisation, reuse-distance prediction, and critical-path
awareness to achieve lossless KV-cache offloading across GPU, DRAM, and SSD.
Our measurement study across three model families reveals that attention
patterns concentrate heavily (Gini $> 0.91$) and exhibit stable hot-set
membership (Jaccard up to 0.90), enabling an EMA predictor (Jaccard = 0.66)
that outperforms InfiniGen's cross-layer approach (0.46).  The critical-path
model ensures that promotion latency (P50 = 46\,$\mu$s, P99 = 250\,$\mu$s)
is fully hidden behind decode compute (25.4\,ms per step).

In our evaluation across 1{,}350 prompts, \sys{} achieves 100\% bit-exact
output match with full-GPU serving, maintains service at 10\% GPU KV budget
with only 7\% throughput loss, and adds only 1.1\% overhead when enabling
the SSD tier.  The $N{=}10$ sampling mode achieves 89\% classification
accuracy with approximately zero overhead.

Future directions include multi-node tiered caching for disaggregated
serving architectures, integration with KV quantisation techniques for
a four-tier hierarchy (GPU/DRAM/SSD/quantised), and co-design with
sparse attention kernels such as Quest~\cite{tang2024quest} to combine
residency scheduling with compute-level optimisation.

\section{Offload Safety Condition}\label{app:proof}

\begin{theorem}[Offload Safety]
If block $b$'s predicted reuse distance $\hat{r}_b$ satisfies
$\hat{r}_b > \lceil \ell_T(b) / \Delta \rceil + m$ with $m \geq 1$, and the
prediction error $|\hat{r}_b - r_b| < m$ (where $r_b$ is the true reuse
distance), then the promotion transfer initiated at step
$t + \hat{r}_b - \lceil \ell_T / \Delta \rceil$ will complete before step
$t + r_b$, i.e., before the block is next accessed.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The promotion transfer takes at most $\ell_T(b)$ time.  With the decode-step
compute time $\Delta$, the transfer spans at most
$\lceil \ell_T(b) / \Delta \rceil$ decode steps.  The promotion is initiated
$\hat{r}_b - \lceil \ell_T / \Delta \rceil$ steps after offload, meaning
it completes by step $t + \hat{r}_b$.  Since $\hat{r}_b \leq r_b + m - 1$
(from the prediction error bound) and $\hat{r}_b > \lceil \ell_T / \Delta
\rceil + m$, the transfer completes at step $t + \hat{r}_b \leq t + r_b +
m - 1$, which is at most $m - 1$ steps before the actual access at step
$t + r_b$.  Since $m \geq 1$, the promotion completes on or before the step
the block is needed.
\end{proof}
